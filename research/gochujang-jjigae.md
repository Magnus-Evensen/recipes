## Canonical definition

A *jjigae* (찌개 — a thick, salty stew built to be eaten with rice, more solids than broth) whose
seasoning is led by 고추장 (*gochujang*, fermented chilli paste). The standard build is meat fried
with the paste, liquid added, then potato, 애호박 (*aehobak*, Korean courgette), onion, mushrooms
and tofu, finished with green chilli and 대파 (*daepa*, Korean leek) or spring onion. Pork is the
default meat in Korean home recipes; beef, clams, canned tuna and Spam all appear.

**What makes it this dish and not a neighbouring one:**

- **Against 된장찌개 (*doenjang jjigae*):** same pot, same vegetables, same fried-paste start. The
  line is which paste leads. Most gochujang jjigae carries some doenjang (1 part to 2–6 parts
  gochujang); flip the ratio and it's a doenjang jjigae with a bit of chilli.
- **Against 짜글이 (*jjageuli*):** same ingredients cooked down with a fraction of the liquid until
  it's closer to a braise. One source here (180 ml stock for 2) is really a jjageuli and calls
  itself one in its own notes. Standard jjigae sits around **0.6 ml liquid per gram of solids**.
- **Against 애호박찌개 (*aehobak jjigae*):** often the same dish named after its main vegetable.
  Korean Bapsang's version is titled both ways. Courgette-led versions are a summer thing in Korea.
- **The fermented-soybean component is a supporting note, not a definer.** 7 of 11 sources add
  doenjang; the other 4 (both Aaron & Claire versions, Paik Jong-won's, one 10000recipe author)
  don't, and nobody treats its absence as a different dish.

### Doenjang vs. miso

Both are fermented soybean pastes, but traditional doenjang is soybeans and salt only, fermented
from meju blocks, while miso adds a koji starter grown on rice or barley. The practical result:
**doenjang is saltier, sharper and funkier; miso is milder and sweeter**, white miso most of all,
red miso closest to doenjang. Factory doenjang often uses koji too and sits between the two.

**The miso in use is Hikari white miso**, a light-coloured *shinshu*-style miso rather than a
sweet *saikyo* miso. Its EU label gives **11.5% salt**, the same as doenjang's survey average and
twice gochujang's. Swapping it in for gochujang roughly doubles the salt of the swapped portion.

So "miso in place of doenjang" is a genuine substitution, not a like-for-like swap. The ratio
being used — a bit of gochujang replaced — is exactly the role doenjang plays in 7 of 11 sources.
What changes is the character of that layer: rounder and sweeter with miso, earthier with
doenjang.

### Salt

Reference figures used below: Korean nationwide survey averages of **gochujang 5.7%, doenjang
11.0%, soy (ganjang) 14.7%** salt. CJ beef dasida label: **44.1%**. Fish sauce (~23%) and
gukganjang (~20%) are **assumptions, not verified** — product labels vary a lot and should replace
these. Korea's food safety ministry (MFDS) sets **≤1.0% salt for stews**; measured home stews in a
Seoul city test ranged 0.7% (kimchi jjigae) to 1.3% (doenjang jjigae).

Built-in salt, as a share of the whole pot before any "salt to taste":

| Source | Seasoning | Pot (est.) | Built-in salt |
|---|---|---|---|
| Korean Bapsang (2 servings) | 1.5 Tbsp gochujang, ½ Tbsp doenjang, 480 ml water | ~1.15 kg | **~0.2%** — an outlier; reads under-seasoned on paper |
| A&C 2024, beef (6 servings) | 3 Tbsp gochujang, 1 Tbsp soy, 1 Tbsp fish sauce, 1 Tbsp dasida, 1.44 L | ~3.7 kg | **~0.38%**, then salt to taste |
| A&C 2021, pork (2–3) | 1.5 Tbsp gochujang, ½ Tbsp soy, ½ Tbsp oyster sauce, ½ Tbsp dasida, 720 ml | ~1.5 kg | **~0.39%**, then salt to taste |
| Paik Jong-won, via 10000recipe (2) | 2 Tbsp gochujang, 3 Tbsp gukganjang, 600 ml | ~1.7 kg | **~0.7%** — most of it from gukganjang |

Two things come out of this. **In A&C 2024, dasida is the single biggest salt source** (~4.4 g of
~14 g). Dropping it for a scratch stock without replacing that salt will make the stew flat. And
the Korean-language versions season harder than A&C before tasting, with gukganjang carrying the
load rather than salt.

## Base recipe

A synthesis of the Korean-language sources, for ~4 servings (pot ≈ 2.5 kg).

**Anchovy-kelp stock** (~1 L):

- 1.2 L cold water
- 15 g dried anchovies (*myeolchi*), heads and guts removed, toasted dry for a minute
- 10 × 10 cm kombu

Bring to a simmer, pull the kombu after ~5 min, simmer the anchovies 10 min more, strain.

**Stew:**

- 400 g pork shoulder or neck, bite-sized
- 1 Tbsp neutral oil (skip if using belly)
- 3 cloves garlic, minced
- 3 Tbsp gochujang (54 g)
- 1 Tbsp doenjang (17 g)
- 1½ Tbsp gochugaru
- 1 L anchovy-kelp stock (or second-rinse rice water)
- 2 medium potatoes (~300 g), 1.5 cm pieces
- 1 aehobak or courgette (~300 g), half-moons 1 cm
- 1 onion, chunked
- 150 g mushrooms
- 300 g firm or medium-firm tofu, cubed
- 1–2 green chillies (*cheongyang* if available), 1 daepa or 2 spring onions
- 1 Tbsp gukganjang + 1 Tbsp anchovy or fish sauce, then gukganjang to taste

**Method:**

1. Brown the pork over medium-high until coloured and the fat is running.
2. Heat to low. Garlic, gochujang, doenjang and gochugaru in; fry 2–3 min until darkened and the
   oil turns red. Splash of stock if it catches.
3. Stock, gukganjang and fish sauce in. Boil, then potato and onion; ~10 min until the potato is
   nearly tender.
4. Courgette, mushrooms, tofu and chilli; 5–8 min until the courgette is soft.
5. Taste. Season with gukganjang toward ~0.8% (≤1.0%). Leek or spring onion in, off the heat.

Built-in salt ≈ 12 g → ~0.5% of the pot; the gukganjang at the end does the rest.

**Reasoning on the contested calls:**

- **Doenjang at 1:3.** The median of the sources that use it. It's the authentic version of the
  move already being made with miso.
- **Gochugaru fried with the paste.** The majority approach and the one already cooked. Paik's
  later addition is the alternative to test.
- **Anchovy-kelp stock over dasida.** Four Korean sources use it; it's scratch, 15 min, and pairs
  with pork as a standard combination. It carries no salt, so seasoning has to make up for it.
- **Gukganjang as the finishing salt.** It's what Korean sources reach for. It seasons with less
  colour and sweetness than regular soy.
- **~0.6 ml liquid per g solids.** Matches both A&C 2024 and Paik; much less and it's a jjageuli.

**Sourcing (Nørrebro):** gochujang, miso, fish sauce, soy, kombu in stock. Doenjang, gukganjang,
dried anchovies, gochugaru, aehobak and tofu from the bazar.

## My deviations

**Before this file, several cooks combining A&C's 2024 recipe and Korean Bapsang's**, with pork
instead of beef and part of the gochujang replaced with miso (Hikari white) for balance and depth.
Filling: potato, mushrooms, courgette, onion, pork, tofu, spring onion. Pork browned hard over
high heat for flavour and texture. Quantities and cut not recorded.

## Open questions

1. **Product salt.** Read the labels of the gochujang, miso, fish sauce and soy in use. Every salt
   figure here rests on survey averages or assumptions.
2. **Which miso?** White and red miso pull in opposite directions. Worth one cook with doenjang
   at the same ratio, side by side if possible.
3. **Gochugaru timing.** Fried with the paste vs. added with the liquid (Paik). Does frying add
   depth or bitterness?
4. **Stock.** Anchovy-kelp vs. dasida vs. rice water. Does scratch stock earn its 15 minutes in a
   pot this heavily seasoned?
5. **Final salt.** Does it want ~0.65% or closer to 0.8–1.0% like Korean home stews?
6. **Next day.** One Korean poster says it's commonly thought better the next day. Potato and tofu
   make it a poor freezer candidate; fridge for 2–3 days.
7. **Sugar.** Needed at all with onion and gochujang's own sweetness?
8. **Paste concentration.** A&C 2024 uses roughly half the paste per kilo of pot that the Korean
   sources do. If the stew tastes thin once salt is right, raise the paste before anything else.
