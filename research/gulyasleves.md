## Read this first — you may have asked for a different dish

In Hungary, **gulyás is a soup**. The thick, short-sauced beef-and-paprika stew that most of
Europe calls "goulash" is **pörkölt**, and Hungarian sources treat confusing the two as the
classic foreigner's error. The Hungarian Museum of Trade and Tourism draws the line explicitly:
gulyás sits in the soup category, pörkölt and paprikás are ragù-type dishes. Pörkölt takes no
potato, and — this is the useful detail — **no caraway and no garlic**, which are exactly the two
things that make a gulyás taste like a gulyás.

The four-pillar framing (gulyás / pörkölt / paprikás / tokány) comes from George Lang and is
repeated everywhere:

| | Cut | Paprika | Liquid | Distinguishing move |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Gulyás** | cubes | yes | soup-length | potato, caraway, garlic, root veg, csipetke |
| **Pörkölt** | cubes | yes | short, sauce-like | repeated braise-and-fry-back, nothing else added |
| **Paprikás** | cubes | yes | short, creamy | finished with soured cream slaked with flour |
| **Tokány** | strips | usually not | short | black pepper and marjoram instead of paprika |

This file researches the soup. If what you actually want is the stew, say so and I'll do
`research/marhaporkolt.md` — it's a genuinely different file, not a thickness setting, and it
would be wrong to fold them together.

## Canonical definition

Beef cut in cubes, cooked down in a lard-and-onion-and-paprika base until tender, then extended
with water or light stock into a soup carrying root vegetables, potato and usually csipetke
(pinched egg-flour dough). Caraway and garlic are non-negotiable. Herdsmen's food from the Alföld,
cooked in a **bogrács** over wood; the domestic stove version is the same dish with less smoke.

The dish is unusually stable across Hungary — Wikipedia notes it varies little regionally compared
with most national dishes, and the corpus bears that out. The disagreements below are about
proportion and sequence, not identity.

## Variation matrix

| Axis | Consensus | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cut | Shin / shank (*marhalábszár*) — 6/7 sources | Neck (*marhanyak*), chuck, leg | Collagen is the point. Fördős says leave the bluish silverskin on and only trim hard fat and gristle — it dissolves and improves the broth |
| Protein | Beef | **Pork shoulder (*sertéslapocka*) is a fully established parallel tradition**, not a lesser version; chicken also named | Hungarian pork versions run 90 min–2 h against beef's 3–3.5. One Hungarian site argues the pork version is the more popular one on consistency and value |
| Onion : meat | 25–35% of meat weight | 1 tbsp-scale token amounts up to **1:1 by weight** in bogrács practice | The big-onion end is the older, more rustic reading. It dissolves entirely and is the body of the soup |
| Fat | Lard (*sertészsír*), 60–100 g/kg meat | Goose or duck fat; fat rendered from diced *szalonna* (smoked *kolozsvári* in several pork versions); oil | Oil is common in modern recipes and contested — the argument is that paprika's flavour compounds release properly into animal fat. Váncsa's line is szalonna fat or nothing |
| Paprika timing | **Off the heat**, stirred in, liquid within seconds | A minority adds it later to already-wet food specifically to avoid the risk | Total agreement that scorched paprika turns bitter and the dish is unrecoverable. This is the one step everyone warns about |
| Paprika quantity | ~1 heaped tbsp per 600 g meat (≈1.5–2% of meat weight) | Up to 2 tbsp per 500 g | Quality dominates quantity. Szegedi or kalocsai, bought recently |
| Caraway | Yes, ~1 tsp ground per 600 g | Whole vs ground; Fördős uses a heaped tbsp ground, which is a lot | With garlic, the gulyás/pörkölt tell |
| Green pepper + tomato | Yes, 1 each — flavour base, not bulk | Fördős fries them **first**, before the onion, until the tomato browns; most add them to the finished base | Hungarian *TV paprika* or *cecei fehérpaprika*; a pale, thin-walled sweet pepper |
| Root veg | Carrot + parsley root, celeriac common | Kohlrabi, lovage (*lestyán*) | In at roughly two-thirds of the way through, not at the start |
| Potato | Yes — last ~25 minutes | Omitted in named variants (Székely, bean, Csángó) | Overcooked potato clouding the soup is the failure mode |
| Csipetke | Yes | "Plain style" omits it in favour of more vegetables | 100 g flour : 1 egg : pinch of salt, rested, pinched, dried briefly, 2–3 min in the soup |
| Browning the meat | **No hard sear.** Meat goes into the base and is cooked "until it whitens" | A few bogrács recipes *átpörkölik* the meat harder first | See below — this is the one place your instincts and the tradition disagree |
| Liquid | Water; better sources use a stock from the trimmings | ~300 ml per person of finished soup | Fördős builds the base with stock from the start so the meat cooks in concentrated flavour |

### On not searing

Your other braises (carnitas, the bolognese) start with a hard sear. Hungarian gulyás does not,
and it isn't laziness. The technique is **párolás–zsírjára pirítás**: add a little liquid, cook it
away, let the base fry back until the fat visibly separates, repeat. Each cycle browns the base
rather than the meat surface, and it's done several times. The museum describes exactly this loop
for both gulyás and pörkölt.

You'd get more Maillard from searing. You'd also get fond that then has to survive hours of soup,
and you'd break the sequence where paprika goes into moderate-temperature fat. My call is to
follow the tradition, because the cycling *is* the technique and skipping it to sear instead is
substituting one browning mechanism for another rather than adding to it. But it's a clean A/B to
run once.

## Base recipe

The synthesis of the tradition, with beef. What I'd actually cook is the pork version under **My
deviations** below — this section stays beef so the file keeps recording what the dish is.

Six servings. Dutch oven, induction. Roughly 4 hours, most of it unattended.

**Meat and base**
- 1 kg beef shin, 3 cm cubes (they halve in size — don't go smaller)
- 300 g yellow onion, finely diced
- 80 g lard
- 5 cloves garlic, crushed
- 20 g sweet Hungarian paprika
- 4 g caraway, ground fresh from whole
- 1 sweet pointed pepper, diced
- 1 tomato, diced
- Hot paprika or a fresh chilli, to taste

**Soup**
- 2 carrots, 1 parsley root, 100 g celeriac — all in 1 cm dice
- 600 g potato, 2 cm dice
- ~2.5 l water or light stock

**Csipetke**
- 100 g plain flour, 1 egg, 2 g salt

**Method**

1. Trim hard fat and gristle from the shin; leave the silverskin. If you want a stock, simmer the
   trimmings and the vegetable peel in 2 l water while you work.
2. Sweat the onion in the lard over medium-low heat until it collapses completely — 20–30 minutes,
   not 5. It should be soft enough to disappear into the soup later.
3. Add the pepper and tomato, cook until the tomato darkens. Add the garlic, 1 minute.
4. **Off the heat.** Paprika and caraway in, stir through, and a ladle of liquid in within a few
   seconds. Back on the heat.
5. Add the meat and salt. Cook, stirring, until the surfaces turn pale — no browning.
6. Now cycle: barely cover with liquid, simmer until it's nearly gone and the fat separates out
   red at the edges, add liquid again. Three to four hours at a low simmer, stirring every 20
   minutes or so. Done when a cube gives completely under a spoon.
7. Root vegetables in, 15 minutes. Top up with hot liquid to soup length — about 300 ml per person
   — and bring back to a simmer.
8. Potato in for the last 25 minutes.
9. Csipetke: knead flour, egg and salt into a stiff dough, rest 10 minutes, pinch irregular
   fingernail-sized pieces onto a floured tray, let them dry while the potato cooks. Into the soup
   for the final 2–3 minutes.

### Calls made, and why

**Salt.** The 1–1.5%-of-meat rule doesn't transfer here and applying it blind will leave this
badly under-seasoned. 12 g on 1 kg of meat is right for a pörkölt; in a finished soup weighing
close to 4 kg it's 0.3%. Salt in two stages: **~10 g with the meat at step 5** (seasons the meat
itself during the long cook), then bring the finished soup to **0.8–0.9% of total weight** — weigh
the pot empty first and do the arithmetic rather than guessing. Expect another 20–25 g. Taste
before committing the last 5 g; potato and csipetke both absorb salt after they go in.

**Paprika sourcing is the real constraint,** not technique. Supermarket paprika that's been in the
cupboard eight months will make a dull brown soup no matter how well it's cooked. This dish is
~2% paprika by meat weight and tastes of exactly one thing. The bazar is the better bet than the
supermarket; look for szegedi or kalocsai, *édesnemes* grade, in a small quantity.

**Lard over oil.** Danish supermarkets carry *svinefedt*. The Hungarian argument about paprika
releasing into animal fat may or may not survive scrutiny, but it costs nothing to test the
traditional version first.

**No probe needed.** This is a feel dish — the cube collapsing under a spoon is the signal, same
as the carnitas.

## My deviations

### Pork instead of beef

**What:** pork shoulder in place of beef shin, plus a pork hock and smoked bacon.

**Why:** beef sits badly, and beef is expensive here. Honest note on the second reason — shin is
one of the cheapest beef cuts, so the saving is smaller than "beef vs pork" implies generally. The
intolerance carries this decision on its own; cost is a bonus.

**Authenticity cost: near zero.** *Sertésgulyás* from *sertéslapocka* is a parallel Hungarian
tradition with its own body of recipes, not a substitution. It stays a gulyás.

**What it actually costs: body.** Shin is the most collagen-dense cut on the animal and that
gelatine is the weight of the soup in the mouth. Shoulder has less. Everything below is either
recovering that or working around pork's shorter window.

**Changes to the ingredients**
- **1.2 kg pork shoulder**, trimmed to ~1 kg, 3 cm cubes. Trim harder than for carnitas — shoulder
  renders a lot of fat, and at soup length that reads greasy rather than rich.
- **1 pork hock** (*svineskank*) in with the meat. Cheap, and it restores collagen and depth in
  one move. Pull the meat off it at the end and return it to the pot; discard bone and skin.
- **80–100 g smoked streaky bacon**, diced, rendered first — drop the lard to ~40 g to compensate.
  This is the authentic compensation for the missing beef backbone: several Hungarian pork
  versions start from rendered *füstölt szalonna*. Not a patch invented to fill the gap.
- Everything else unchanged. Paprika stays at 20 g, salt logic unchanged.

**Changes to the method**
- Step 2 becomes: render the bacon in the lard until it gives up its fat, then the onion goes in
  on top and sweats down as before. Leave the bacon in.
- **Step 6: 75 minutes to 2 hours, not 3 to 4.** This is the change that matters most. Beef shin
  forgives overcooking — the collagen keeps converting. Pork shoulder has a window, and past it
  the fibres go stringy and dry even sitting in liquid. Start checking at 75 minutes. Same
  doneness signal, but you're catching it rather than waiting it out.
- Total cook drops to roughly 2.5 hours. Still a weekend dish, no longer an all-afternoon one.

**Make it the day before.** Worth doing anyway, and here it does double duty: chilled overnight,
the rendered pork fat sets on top and lifts off in one piece. Cleanest fix for the greasiness, and
the soup is better on day two regardless.

### Freezer

Freezes well, but **freeze before step 7**. Potato goes mealy and csipetke goes to mush after a
thaw; the meat-and-base stage is the part worth keeping. Scale to ~1.8 kg of shoulder, eat six
portions fresh, freeze the rest in 700–800 ml batches, then thaw, extend with water, and add fresh
vegetables and csipetke on the day. That should beat fresh for the same reason the carnitas does —
the base keeps improving and only the perishable parts are redone.

### Gut health

One line: long-cooked collagen, and otherwise mostly onion, root vegetables and water. Good on
that axis. No change to the recipe on that account.

### What actually got cooked — 2026-11-05

Four servings. The hock, the parsley root, the csipetke and the lard all came out before cooking,
and the result was good enough to graduate. Those were four things specified above that the dish
turned out not to need, which says the version written here was padded — partly out of the corpus,
partly hedging a first cook. The lean version is the baseline now; `recipes/gulyasleves.md` is it.

- **No hock.** Bacon fat alone carried the base.
- **No lard.** 70 g of smoked streaky renders enough to fry the onion. A spoon of neutral oil
  covers a lean batch.
- **No parsley root, celeriac in.** Not missed.
- **No csipetke.** Not missed either, with 400 g of potato already in the pot.
- **Vegetable order corrected.** The 15-minute head start for the root vegetables in the base
  recipe above is wrong: a 2 cm potato cube and a 1 cm carrot dice are closer in cooking time than
  that, and the stagger overcooked the carrot. Five minutes is the right gap.

**Salt — the base recipe's figure was wrong.** 0.8% of finished weight is too high for this soup,
for two reasons: it assumes an unsalted base, and 70 g of cured smoked bacon both adds salt
directly and amplifies the perception of it. The weigh-the-pot method was also unusable in
practice — a 0.1 g coffee scale cannot weigh a four-kilo Dutch oven, which is a kitchen fact that
should have ruled the method out before it was written down.

**Empirical figure: ~8 g total for four servings, about 2 g per serving.** 10 g read slightly
salty. The recipe file splits it 6 g on the meat and ~2 g to finish, which is only ~0.6% of meat
weight at the first stage — deliberately below the 1–1.5% braise rule, because the bacon is
covering the difference. Scale per serving, taste at the end, and don't weigh the pot.

## Open questions

- **Sear or don't.** Worth cooking the traditional way first, then A/B-ing a seared batch. My
  guess is the sear is a small gain for a real disruption to the paprika step, but it's a guess.
- **Does the hock actually close the gap?** Still open, and the first cook did not settle it. The
  soup did not feel thin without the hock — the onion, pepper and tomato broke down into the broth
  and gave it mouthfeel on their own, which is the *párolás–zsírjára pirítás* cycling working
  without any collagen at all. But that pot ran short on liquid (the salt arithmetic proves it), so
  what was tested was "no hock at high concentration", not "no hock". At proper soup length it
  might still matter. May never need answering, since the short version is the better soup.
- ~~**Is the smoked bacon too much?**~~ **Answered: no.** Paprika dominated, bacon did not shout.
  70 g per four servings stands. This also retires the worry about dull-looking paprika — the
  bazar's orange-rather-than-red powder was fine, and the smell test is the one that predicts the
  result, not colour through a plastic bag.
- **Onion at 30% vs 100%.** The 1:1 bogrács ratio is the older tradition and it's a large enough
  jump that it probably changes the dish, not just the body. Untested.
- **Frying the pepper and tomato first** (Fördős) against adding them to the finished base. A
  single-variable test, cheap to run.
- **Caraway quantity.** Fördős's heaped tablespoon per 600 g is four times most recipes. Worth
  knowing whether that's the point of the dish or a personal excess.
- ~~**Whether the csipetke earns its place**~~ **Answered: it doesn't**, at least not alongside
  400 g of potato. Omitted and not missed.
- **Liquid at proper soup length.** The first cook ran short and was better for it. Worth cooking
  once at the full ~250–300 ml per person to know whether the concentration is doing the work the
  hock was supposed to do.
- **What to do about `recipes/goulash.md`**, the June 2026 entry — a different, unsourced take on
  the same dish, and the site now carries both.
