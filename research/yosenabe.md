## Canonical definition

A pot of dashi-based broth on a tabletop burner, into which meat or fish, tofu, mushrooms and
vegetables are added in rounds and eaten as they cook. The name is literal: 寄せ (*yose*, to gather)
plus 鍋 (*nabe*, pot). Japanese recipe sources describe it as the pot you can put anything in, and it
ranked first nationally in Weathernews's 2025 nabe survey.

**What makes it this dish and not a neighbouring one:**

- **The broth is seasoned before anything goes in.** Soy, mirin, sake and salt are in the pot from
  the start, and the broth itself is meant to be good enough to drink. That is the one firm line.
- **Against ちり鍋 (*chirinabe*), 常夜鍋 (*jōyanabe*) and しゃぶしゃぶ (*shabu-shabu*):** same table,
  same burner, opposite logic. These use plain kombu dashi (常夜鍋 adds sake), and all the seasoning
  comes from the ponzu bowl. 常夜鍋 is the closest neighbour to what was cooked here: pork and a leafy
  green in kombu-and-sake broth, dipped in ponzu, named because you don't tire of it even eaten every night.
- **The boundary is blurry in practice.** Mainstream Japanese recipes routinely serve ponzu with
  yosenabe too, and lightly seasoned versions shade into chirinabe. The working distinction is
  **how much salt is in the broth**, which turns out to be the main axis of the dish (see matrix).
- **ミルフィーユ鍋 (mille-feuille nabe)** — napa and pork packed in layers — is a presentation of the
  same ingredients, not a different broth logic. It's built once rather than fed in rounds.
- **鍋 itself** is the umbrella (*nabemono*); yosenabe is the generic, unspecialised member. Named
  regional pots (Akita's きりたんぽ鍋, Fukuoka's 水炊き) have fixed ingredients and broths; yosenabe
  doesn't.

## Base recipe

For 2 (generous), in one pot on a tabletop burner.

**Broth** (~1.3 L):

- 1.2 L dashi — scratch (12 g kombu, 20 g katsuobushi) or instant
- 45 ml soy sauce — Japanese koikuchi. Chinese light soy (Lee Kum Kee) is usually saltier per
  spoon; check the label and scale down if so
- 30 ml mirin
- 30 ml sake
- ¾ tsp fine salt (4.5 g) — **with scratch dashi only.** With salted granules at a full packet dose,
  start with no added salt and taste

Target ~0.9% salt before anything goes in.

**Pot:**

- 350–400 g pork shoulder, sliced 2–3 mm across the grain from part-frozen
- ½ head napa cabbage, stems cut 3 cm, leaves cut 5 cm
- 1 pack enoki, split into small clumps
- 4–6 shiitake, stems removed
- 1 block firm or silken tofu (300–400 g), cubed
- 1 leek, sliced diagonally 1 cm
- 1 carrot, sliced thin (optional)
- Ponzu for dipping (optional at this salt level)

**Method:**

1. Freeze the pork 1–1.5 h until firm but not solid, then slice.
2. Make the dashi. Scratch: kombu soaked 30+ min, heated to just below a boil and removed;
   katsuobushi in, 1 min simmer, 2 min steep, strain.
3. Season the broth in the table pot and taste it. It should taste like a soup you'd happily drink.
4. First round, lid on, ~5 min at a simmer: napa stems, carrot, leek, shiitake, tofu, some pork.
5. Second round: napa leaves and enoki, cooked until just wilted. Eat; feed the pot in rounds.
6. Skim foam as it appears. Taste once or twice — the napa releases water and the broth weakens.

**Reasoning on the contested calls:**

- **~0.9% rather than ~0.5%.** The first cook at 0.5% was bland, and every source that drinks the
  broth sits higher. 0.9% is the low end of the standard range on purpose: the broth only gets
  weaker as the napa releases water, so it's easier to add salt than recover from too much.
- **1.2 L rather than 1.5 L.** The first cook started with 1.5 L and finished with 1.5–2 L. The
  vegetables put more water in than the simmer took out. Napa wilts down, so it doesn't need to be
  submerged from the start.
- **Change one variable.** Mirin and sake are kept where they were. Salt is the only thing that
  moved, so the second cook tests one change.
- **Granules vs. scratch.** A full packet dose of regular granules for 1.2 L can carry ~4–5 g of
  salt on its own — around a third of the target. That's why the added salt is conditional. The
  first cook's granule brand and dose weren't recorded, so the true salt that night is unknown.
- **Pork shoulder.** Japanese supermarkets sell shoulder (肩ロース) sliced for shabu-shabu; it's a
  normal choice, not a substitution. Belly is the other common cut and is fattier.

**Sourcing (Nørrebro):** kombu and soy in stock. Katsuobushi, mirin, sake, ponzu, enoki, shiitake
and tofu from the bazar.

## My deviations

**First cook, 2026-11-01.** Weeknight version.

- Dashi from **instant pellets** (brand and dose not recorded). Plan is pellets on weeknights,
  scratch kombu-katsuo at weekends.
- Broth: 1.5 L, seasoned with 30 ml soy, 30 ml mirin, 30 ml sake, ½ tsp salt — ~0.5% before
  counting the pellets. **Not salty at all.** Needs to go up.
- **500 g pork shoulder** sliced 2–3 mm from part-frozen: good thickness. Ate about two-thirds
  between two people, so ~350 g is right for a single dinner.
- **No top-up needed.** 1.5–2 L broth left over, against the expectation that it would reduce.
  The original plan assumed the broth would get saltier as it went; it got weaker instead.
- Leftover meat, vegetables and broth kept for breakfast the next day; broth kept for zosui
  the day after.

## Open questions

1. **Which pellets, how much, and which soy?** Needed to know the real salt of the first cook and to
   write a separate weeknight seasoning line. All salt figures here assume Japanese soy at 14.5%.
2. **Is ~0.9% right?** Second cook should settle it. If the broth is drunk and ponzu barely used,
   it may want to go to ~1.1%.
3. **Ponzu or no ponzu?** At a properly seasoned broth it should be optional. Check whether it
   still earns its place — that's the chirinabe-vs-yosenabe question in practice.
4. **Zosui from leftover broth** — untested. Rinse the rice for the loose, soupy result; taste and
   season the broth first, since it'll have weakened rather than concentrated.
5. **Udon vs. rice finish.** Udon is the national majority; try it once to compare.
6. **Scratch dashi vs. pellets** — does the weekend version taste meaningfully better in a pot
   this loaded with other flavour?
