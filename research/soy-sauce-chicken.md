## Canonical definition

A whole young chicken cooked gently in a seasoned soy liquid until just done, cooled, then chopped
and served with a little of the reduced liquid poured over. The meat stays pale inside; only the
skin takes the deep mahogany colour. The name is literal — 豉油 is Cantonese for soy sauce, 油雞
(*yau gai*) is the common short form, and the "oil" has nothing to do with cooking oil.

**What makes it this dish and not a neighbouring one:**

- **The chicken is never boiled and never browned.** No searing, no roasting, no frying. The liquid
  is held below a simmer and the bird is cooked by immersion and residual heat. If it boils, the
  skin splits and the breast goes stringy.
- **The soy is a cooking medium, not a glaze or a marinade.** The bird goes into a hot, already-made
  liquid; the flavour penetrates a few millimetres and the interior stays mild. A dish where soy is
  brushed on and the chicken is roasted is a different animal.
- **It is a 燒味 (*siu mei*) item by shop convention but not by method** — Hong Kong roast-meat shops
  sell it alongside char siu and roast duck, next to 白切雞, though neither of those two is roasted.
- **Against 白切雞 (white cut chicken):** same poaching geometry, opposite intent. 白切雞 is plain
  water and reveals the bird; 豉油雞 covers it. The trade sources are blunt that this is exactly why
  the technique exists — the seasoned soy masks the chicken, so 豉油雞 was historically made with the
  less-fresh bird while the good one went to 白切雞. Refrigeration has since erased the distinction
  in shops, but the logic is still the reason this dish is forgiving.
- **Against 滷水雞 (*lou seui gai*, master-stock chicken):** genuinely blurry, and the two names get
  swapped on menus. The working distinction is that a 滷水 is a maintained, long-lived, heavily
  spiced master stock in which many things are cooked, whereas a 豉油雞 liquid is soy-dominant, often
  built fresh, and lighter on spice. In practice 豉油雞 liquid gets kept and reused, so it drifts
  toward being a 滷水 over time. This is a continuum, not a boundary.
- **玫瑰豉油雞 / 玫瑰油雞 (rose soy chicken)** is the same dish with 玫瑰露酒 — a rose-scented distilled
  sorghum spirit, originally from Tianjin — in the liquid. It is a named sub-variant, not a
  different dish.

## Variation matrix

| Axis | Consensus | Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cooking geometry | Bird cooked in the soy liquid, held at a bare simmer, turned | Full submersion (shops) → half-covered and turned → heavy lidded pot relying on retained heat → rice cooker | Full submersion needs 2–3 L of liquid. The lidded-heavy-pot method is a documented home workaround specifically to avoid buying that much soy |
| Soy : water | ~1:2 | 1:1.3 to 1:2.5 | Unusually tight agreement across Hong Kong, Guangdong and Taiwan sources. This is a pillar |
| Soy composition | Light soy (生抽) as the base, dark soy (老抽) at roughly a quarter to a sixth of it | 1:3 to 1:6 light:dark; some HK versions add Indonesian sweet soy | Dark soy is for colour only. Serious shops advertise 頭抽 (first-draw light soy) as the base |
| Sugar | Rock sugar, generously | ~50–130 g per litre of liquid; slab sugar (黃片糖) or sweet soy as alternatives | Explicitly there to blunt the salt, not to sweeten. The low end tastes savoury, the high end approaches a 燒味 shop's sweetness |
| The wine | Present in nearly every version, often added late | 玫瑰露酒 (defines the 玫瑰 variant) · Shaoxing/花雕 as the standard substitute · one Taiwanese source argues any distilled spirit does the job | Aromatic, not functional. Added at the end preserves more of it |
| Spice and aromatics | Ginger and spring onion always | Nothing else at all (home) → star anise + cassia + bay → the six-spice 燒臘 base (star anise, 草果, clove, dried tangerine peel, 沙姜, liquorice) → 20-ingredient commercial 滷水 | Shallots are common in Cantonese home versions. Home cooks omitting the spice bag entirely is a real, widespread position, not a shortcut |
| Skin prep before cooking | Scald the skin in boiling water, then cold-shock it | Some parboil to clean instead; some skip entirely | Contracts the skin so it doesn't tear during cooking. Cheap insurance |
| Doneness method | Skewer the thigh; clear juices, no blood | Continuous low simmer 25–40 min · simmer 15 min then off-heat covered 15–20 min · turn every 15 min ×4 in a rice cooker | The residual-heat cluster is the majority and exists to stop the breast overshooting while the thigh finishes. Scaling given in one HK source: +5 min at 1.8 kg, −5 min under 1.2 kg |
| After cooking | Rest, then brush repeatedly with strained liquid | Ice bath for crisp skin · maltose glaze (shop practice) · straight to the board | **These conflict.** The ice bath is the 玫瑰豉油雞 route to snappy skin; the repeated brushing is the route to lacquered colour. Sources do one or the other, not both |
| The liquid afterwards | Strain, skim, keep | Fridge 3–4 weeks · frozen and reused 2–3 times · maintained indefinitely as a 滷水 | Also used for soy eggs, ribs, pig trotters. Dilute with water on reuse as it concentrates |
| The bird | Young, tender, 1.2–1.8 kg | 600 g–1.8 kg; one source insists on 三黃雞 or 童子雞 under 1 kg | Explicitly **not** 土雞 / an old bird — it won't cook through in the time and the point isn't chicken flavour anyway |

## Base recipe

For a 1.3–1.5 kg bird in the Dutch oven on induction. Serves 3–4.

**The liquid** (~1.2 L, enough to come roughly two-thirds up the bird in a Dutch oven):

- 320 ml light soy
- 80 ml dark soy
- 800 ml water
- 100 g rock sugar
- 40 ml Shaoxing, or 玫瑰露酒 if the bazar has it — added in the last few minutes
- 6 slices ginger, 3 spring onions knotted, 4 shallots
- 2 star anise, 1 small piece cassia, 2 bay leaves, a strip of dried tangerine peel

**Method:**

1. Simmer everything except the wine for 20–30 minutes to build the liquid before the bird goes near
   it. Several sources make this a separate stage of an hour; 20–30 minutes is the honest floor.
2. Scald the bird: boiling water over the skin, roughly 30 seconds a side, then straight into cold
   water. Dry it.
3. Lower it breast-down into the hot liquid, tilting to fill and empty the cavity two or three times
   so it heats inside and out at the same rate.
4. Hold at a bare simmer — surface trembling, no bubbles breaking — 15 minutes breast-down, lid on.
   Turn, ladle liquid over the exposed side, 15 minutes more.
5. Add the wine, bring briefly back up, kill the heat, and leave covered 20 minutes.
6. Lift out, rest 30 minutes, brushing with strained liquid every 15 minutes.
7. Chop, pour over a few spoons of the reduced liquid.

**Reasoning on the contested calls:**

- **Half-submerged and turned, not fully submerged.** Full immersion is the shop method and gives
  the most even result, but it needs 2–3 L of liquid and therefore around a litre of soy sauce per
  bird. The Dutch oven's retained heat plus turning is the documented substitute and is what the
  heavy-pot sources exist to describe.
- **1:2 soy to water.** This is the one number the sources actually agree on. Don't experiment here
  on the first attempt.
- **100 g rock sugar per 1.2 L** is deliberately mid-range. The 130 g/L end is 燒臘 shop sweetness;
  the 50 g/L end reads plainly salty. Adjust on cycle two.
- **Wine at the end.** Both positions exist in the sources (one Cantonese home cook adds it at the
  start, saying she dislikes a heavy wine note). Late addition keeps more aroma, which is the whole
  reason it's in there.
- **Rest-and-brush rather than ice bath.** The ice bath gives snappier skin; the repeated brushing
  gives the lacquered shop colour. Picking the brushing on the reasoning that a Danish supermarket
  bird has soft, fatty skin that won't reward an ice bath much anyway, and colour is the more
  visible win. This is the most obvious thing to swap on a second attempt.
- **No pre-salting.** The instinct to dry-brine at 1.2% overnight is wrong here — the soy is the
  salt, and a pre-salted bird in a 1:2 soy liquid will come out aggressively salty. The 0.1g scale
  has nothing to do in this dish, which is unusual and worth noticing.
- **Temperature.** Instant-read into the thigh, not the breast. The Cantonese ideal leaves blood
  semi-set at the bone, which sits below where EU guidance puts it; that's a knowing call, not an
  accident. Pulling at 72–74 °C in the thigh is the compromise.
- **Weeknight or not:** roughly 20 min prep + 50 min cook = 70 min, so just over the weeknight
  ceiling on the first pass. With the liquid already made and frozen from a previous batch it drops
  to about 45 minutes, which is comfortably weeknight.

**Sourcing (Nørrebro):** light and dark soy already in stock. Rock sugar, dried tangerine peel,
沙姜 and 草果 from the bazar. Shaoxing is the known gap in `KITCHEN.md` and this is the dish to close
it with; ask for 玫瑰露酒 while there.

**Gut health:** heavily fermented and very high sodium. Notable on that axis, nothing to change.

## Open questions

1. **Ice bath vs. brushing** — the sources treat these as alternatives and never explain the choice.
   Worth testing back to back, since it's the one fork that changes what the finished bird looks and
   feels like.
2. **How much salt does the bird actually take up?** Weighing before and after would give a real
   number and would settle whether the liquid can be diluted further without losing the flavour.
3. **Does the liquid genuinely improve with reuse,** or is that the 滷水 romance leaking across from
   a neighbouring technique? Three cycles should tell.
4. **Breast vs. thigh gap.** The residual-heat method exists to close it. Check whether it actually
   does on a fat-breasted Danish broiler, which has worse proportions than a 三黃雞.
5. **Is the spice bag worth it at all?** A meaningful share of Cantonese home cooks use only ginger,
   spring onion and shallot. Test the minimal version against the six-spice one.
6. **Does the bazar carry 玫瑰露酒?** If not, is Shaoxing actually closer than, say, a neutral
   grain spirit, given one Taiwanese source claims any distilled spirit does the job?
